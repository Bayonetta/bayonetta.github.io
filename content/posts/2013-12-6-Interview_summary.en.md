Since early March this year, I have been shuttling between job fairs one after another. For my own life and future, I have to start preparing seriously.

## Douban

After my first attempt at the NetEase written test, I took part in the Douban interview. Douban was, of course, the company I cared about most. There was a written test at first: two papers, one emphasising fundamental algorithms and learning ability, the other basic Android questions. My answers were reasonably smooth.

About three days later I received a text message from HR informing me that I should attend an interview. Since I was in Wuhan, it was a remote interview.

There were two rounds:

* The first interviewer was Bear, the head of Android, who later became my mentor
* In the second round I met the technical director Qingfeng, an absolute legendary figure (you can search Zhihu for "滴蜡" — yes, thoroughly geeky)

Most of the content related to the projects, so the process went fairly smoothly, and I finally got the offer without trouble.

Since I already had the offer I wanted most, I only attended that single interview until the internship began.

This struck me as quite unreliable.

## Xiaomi

By September I had grown somewhat dissatisfied with my situation as an intern at Douban. The project I was assigned was effectively unsupervised: no PM, no UI, and because the project itself was experimental it rarely received useful user feedback, so I gradually began to lose motivation. On top of that, I was touching a refactor for the first time and, without a mentor's guidance, felt somewhat out of my depth.

<figure>
  <img src="/images/2013-12-06-2.jpg" alt=""/>
  <figcaption>Truly a company worth tens of billions — a very impressive facade, though my lens was terrible and I could not capture the effect at night</figcaption>
</figure>

I received an invitation from Sui Zong at this point, and weighing up my situation I decided to give Xiaomi a try. The department interviewing me was the browser team.

It took about two hours to get there from Douban. Thinking it would be over quickly, I skipped dinner — and then the interview lasted three and a half hours. Poor stomach. Two rounds that evening.

### Round one

Mostly fundamentals, including Android, algorithms, data structures, design patterns and so on.

* Algorithms: find the shortest path in a binary tree (recursive); use a stack to implement a new kind of stack that can return the maximum value (auxiliary space is needed to store the latest maximum after each push)
* Data structures: implement a singly linked list in reverse order
* Design patterns: the singleton pattern

Since my grounding in algorithms was not very solid, and most of my preparation had focused on Android, I did not do especially well on the algorithm questions. Later I found they were all original problems from "Beauty of Programming"; I regretted not preparing in advance.

### Round two

Purely algorithmic. Two questions:

* Fast lookup of names in the contacts list (including initials, full pinyin and full Chinese characters)
* Remove the spaces from a string (without introducing auxiliary space)

### Round three

After the National Day holiday, I was notified to have a simple chat with the head of the browser team and confirm the terms of joining. I really went in expecting a simple chat, and it turned out to be another interview. Underprepared (I had spent the holiday going wild in the northwest), the result was a disaster, and so, quite dramatically, I lost the chance to join Xiaomi. Really it was more my own doing, so for the next month or so, while looking for more opportunities, I also worked on remedying my weaknesses in a targeted way.

## Umeng

When I arrived, the interviewer had gone off to a meeting, so I spent essentially the whole afternoon in idle conversation.

Around dinner time I finally got the interviewer. It took about an hour, and the result was that they needed to discuss it with HR: Umeng might have no campus recruitment plan this year, presumably because the team was not particularly short of people.

There were two questions that left a strong impression:

* The underlying principle of git diff
* Two people in contact by telephone, how to confirm the trustworthiness of the transmitted information (through a trusted third party, or by agreeing on the product of a pair of large primes that cannot be quickly forged)

## Wandou Labs

It arrived far quicker than expected, or perhaps the HR of the other companies were too slow. Having heard that Wandou Labs interviews were very strict, this interview was bound to be gruelling.

### Round one

A fairly deep discussion around my projects, which exposed quite a few hidden risks in real application scenarios caused by insufficient experience. Then an algorithm: traversing and outputting a matrix in clockwise order. Because I had practised writing code on paper too little, it took me quite a while.

There were some problems in the code, the most common and the one I always forget — checking input parameters.

### Round two

I had the honour of meeting Liu Chunyu, formerly of Google, a very friendly man. He too first asked project-related questions, then posed two algorithm problems.

* Given many points on a 2D plane, find a straight line that passes through the most of them
* I have forgotten the specific question, but I recall it required recursively summarising an expression

In fact, the Wandou Labs interview was said to consist of five to seven rounds in total, but my interviews ended there.

Wandou Labs' feedback was that my grounding in algorithms was not solid enough, that I wrote code slowly, and that I was always slow to answer.

## Weico

There was a written test first, then I wrote two demos on the computer. One was WeChat Moments sharing, the other a Weibo Timeline; with limited time I only wrote Moments sharing, and merely outlined an approach for the second. These things were not difficult; they mainly tested practical programming ability, which I had been practising for a long time, so I received the offer without trouble.

## Pudding Mobile

The interview lasted about an hour and a half, with a great many fundamentals, mostly Java, and the interviewer focused more on language characteristics, so someone like me who only cared about practical skills could only sweat. Sigh, I really must finish reading Thinking in Java and how the JVM works.

## Zhihu

Zhihu was another company I considered ideal, and its working environment was good too.

* A chat about Android fundamentals, then I wrote a piece of code converting a number to a string; my code was inefficient, and with the interviewer's guidance I found the problem
* Testing-related matters, and the principle of hash tables. I failed outright

## Summary

The whole experience was fairly gruelling, and the problems it exposed were concentrated in algorithms and Java fundamentals, since companies place more weight on the fundamentals when interviewing.
