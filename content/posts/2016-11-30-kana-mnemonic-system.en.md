After a trip through Kansai, I wanted to visit Japan again more and more. A colleague who was good at Japanese gave me advice, and we used the remaining time after lunch once or twice a week for a small Japanese lesson. I still could not remember the kana chart. It took nearly two months, in bits and pieces, to assemble a method that worked for me.

## A row × column coordinate table

The gojūon chart is a row × column table. The columns are the five vowels (あ, い, う, え, お), and the rows are consonants or semivowels. A row and a column identify a kana: か-row × あ-column gives か, while さ-row × え-column gives せ. The 46 common basic kana fit into this coordinate system. When I forget a shape, I first ask which row and column it belongs to, then reconstruct the sound from the consonant and vowel.

## Etymological associations

Hiragana developed from cursive forms of kanji, while katakana usually took pieces of kanji and was used for phonetic notation and loanwords. For example, hiragana あ comes from the cursive form of 安, か from 加, ぬ from 奴, and れ from 礼. Katakana ア comes from the left side of 阿, イ from the person radical in 伊, カ from 加, and ト from 止.

My “etymological encoding” stores a clue chain instead of a picture: the shape points to its source kanji, and the source kanji points to the row and column. I use four steps:

- Find the source kanji and use it as a unique anchor.
- Trace how the kanji was simplified into the kana.
- Bind the anchor to its row and column; the coordinate supplies the sound.
- Recall in both directions: say the row and sound from the shape, then write the shape from the sound.

## Derived sounds are rules

Voiced sounds, half-voiced sounds, contracted sounds, gemination, and long vowels do not need separate memorization. They are transformations of the 46 basic kana. If I can produce the sound from the rule and recover the basic kana from hearing it, I consider the rule learned.

| Category | Rule | Example | Memory cue |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Voiced | Add dakuten to the か, さ, た, or は rows | `か → が`, `た → だ` | Remember four rows, not twenty new symbols |
| Half-voiced | Add a small circle to the は row | `は → ぱ`, `ふ → ぷ` | Only one row |
| Contracted | Add small `ゃ`, `ゅ`, or `ょ` after an い-row kana | `き + ゃ → きゃ` | It is blending, not a new symbol |
| Geminated | Small `っ` marks a one-beat pause | `まって`, `きって` | A timing rule |
| Long vowel | Extend the vowel; katakana uses `ー` | `おかあさん`, `コーヒー` | Mora count differs between scripts |

## Similar-looking kana

`あ / お`, `ぬ / め / ね`, `る / ろ`, and katakana `シ / ツ`, `ソ / ン` are the easiest to confuse. Repeatedly looking at them only made the confusion more familiar. I wrote a difference list for each pair and reduced the distinction to one feature: stroke direction, crossing point, presence of a loop, or the direction of a dot. Then I practiced with “cover, recall, check”.

After that, I was ready to start proper Japanese at roughly the N5 level. My next step was buying a beginner textbook.
